# Competing hypotheses, explanations and causality

17. Do not interpret the information found in favor of an already accepted hypothesis; record in advance the observation that will force you to revise it.
18. Before drawing a conclusion, formulate at least two plausible explanations and a way to distinguish between them.
19. Look for quality disconfirming evidence, not just confirming examples.
20. Do not consider the absence of disconfirmation as confirmation if the search or measurement was insufficient.
21. Do not present an interpretation as a fact; indicate which observations allow for other explanations.
22. Do not declare a correlation to be a cause without verifying the order of events, confounding factors, and mechanism.
23. Do not explain a person’s behavior with a single personality trait until the situation, stimuli, and constraints have been examined.
24. Do not attribute your own mistake solely to circumstances if the same standard would not be applied to another person.
25. Do not transfer the properties of individual members to the entire group or the group result to each participant.
26. Do not attribute intent, agency, or design to a random coincidence without an independent mechanism.
27. Do not generalize based on apparent successful cases; find excluded, unsuccessful, and ceased cases.
28. Do not accept authority, popularity, position, or the source’s origin instead of verifying the argument itself.
29. Do not trust automated advice, a model, or a tool without verifying the inputs, constraints, and results.
30. Don’t defend an already made choice with a new rationalization; compare it again with the available alternatives.
31. Don’t reject an inconvenient conclusion because of its unpleasantness, or don’t accept a desirable one because of its appeal; check the form of the argument and the premises separately.
91. Don’t test a hypothesis only in the scenario it expects; include a scenario where it should fail.
92. Don’t attribute more merits to the chosen option after the choice; re‑evaluate the alternatives based on the original criteria.
93. Do not select input data based on compliance with expectations; fix the selection rule before viewing the result.
94. Do not let the observer’s expectation change the measurement, labeling, or success threshold; conceal the hypothesis if possible.
95. Do not attribute an effect to the observed object until you have checked whether it arose from the observation process itself.
96. Do not ignore alarming data simply because checking it is unpleasant or inconvenient.
97. Don’t take a vague description for an accurate explanation; demand distinguishing, verifiable characteristics.
98. Don’t assume that a refuted statement automatically has no impact on the conclusion; check whether any traces of it remain in the argumentation.
99. Don’t reject new evidence simply because it contradicts established practice; check the method and reproducibility.
100. Don’t consider someone else’s position more biased than your own without applying the same standard of scrutiny to both.
101. Don’t take your own perception as a neutral picture of reality; look for an independent description of the same facts.
102. Don’t fill gaps in memory with a plausible story; mark them as unknown.
103. Do not call random co-occurrence a causal relationship without comparative data.
104. Do not see an intentional image, face, rule, or message in an ambiguous signal without independent confirmation.
105. Do not explain the properties of a system with human motives if a mechanistic explanation is sufficient.
106. Do not deduce the unchanging essence of a person or group from several signs or actions
107. Do not consider a bad outcome as proof that the world has justly punished the participant; analyze the mechanism and randomness.
108. Do not declare a statement true just because its criticism was erroneous; check the original statement separately.
109. Do not replace the expert’s verification with a reference to their status; assess the applicability of the expertise and the data.
110. Do not accept the majority opinion as evidence without an independent basis.
111. Do not attribute an improvement after an intervention to its effect without comparing it with the natural course and a control.
112. Do not describe the external group as homogeneous; check the variability within it.
113. Don’t jump from similarity to yourself to trust or quality without checking the relevant criteria.
114. Don’t increase the assessment of an object because of the attractiveness of its environment or company.
115. Don’t prefer pleasant data to unpleasant ones; weigh them based on quality and relevance to the issue.
116. Don’t reject someone else’s solution just because it wasn’t created by your team; check its relevance to the task.
117. Don’t devalue an idea just because of its source or the person behind it; evaluate its content before considering its origin.
118. Don’t choose the familiar path just because it’s familiar; compare it with current limitations and alternatives.
