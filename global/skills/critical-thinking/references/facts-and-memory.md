# Sources, memory, salience and input data

1. Don’t judge frequency or probability by how easily an example comes to mind; find the full set of data and the denominator.
2. Don’t take the repetition of a statement as proof of its truth; check the original source or independent confirmation.
3. Don’t confuse familiarity, reading fluency, rhyme, or a beautiful formulation with the reliability of the conclusion.
4. Don’t consider the most noticeable, last, or first fragment to be the most important without comparing it with all relevant data.
5. Don’t generalize based on a vivid, funny, scary, or unusual case; compare it with typical cases.
6. Don’t let a current emotion, sympathy, anxiety, or irritation replace the decision‑making criterion.
7. Don’t mistake the absence of a memory for the absence of an event; look for records, logs, and independent evidence.
8. Don’t trust the memory of the source of the information; separate the fact, the source, the time, and your own interpretation.
9. Don’t transfer the meaning of a statement to another context without checking the conditions, units, and limitations of the original statement.
10. Do not consider a suddenly frequently noticed object or term to have become objectively more common without data on the baseline frequency.
11. Do not substitute the quality of evidence with format, image, text length, or number of details.
12. Do not draw conclusions based on selective recollections of peaks and the end of an experience; reconstruct the entire course of events.
13. Do not draw conclusions about the importance of a problem based on the number of mentions; check the damage, frequency, and scope separately.
14. Don’t assume that an unmentioned risk is non‑existent; check which data is missing and why.
15. Don’t replace fact‑checking with a plausible story; separate the observed from the explanation.
16. Don’t use only sources that are easy to find; consider what types of data could refute the conclusion.
78. Don’t consider an object important just because attention is already focused on it; compare it with the omitted alternatives.
79. Don’t assume that a familiar solution is the best just because you’ve encountered it again.
80. Don’t transfer an assessment from one environment to another without checking what has changed around the object.
81. Don’t draw conclusions about the quality of memory without hints and external supports, if they could have altered the recall.
82. Don’t assume that your current state matches the state of the future user or participant.
83. Don’t consider information more important just because it relates to you personally or to the current task.
84. Do not give negative data more weight than positive data without explaining the difference in reliability.
85. Do not adjust the initial estimate symbolically; recalculate it from independent source data.
86. Do not retain the old opinion just because the new information is unfamiliar; compare the strength of the old and new data.
87. Do not evaluate the difference between options only in direct comparison mode; check their individual value in actual use.
88. Do not let one highlighted feature overshadow the other consequences of the decision.
89. Do not compare monetary values without adjusting for purchasing power, time period, and real consequences.
90. Do not assume that the same absolute difference is equally significant at different scales.
