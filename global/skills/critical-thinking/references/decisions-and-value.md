# Choices, value, costs and consequences

144. Do not take a general description as an individual forecast without specific, distinguishing observations.
145. Do not explain your own behavior by circumstances, and others’ behavior by their character, without a symmetrical check.
146. Do not overestimate control over a random or complex process; separate the action from the result.
147. Do not declare yourself or your work superior to alternatives without an independent rubric and comparable examples.
148. Do not protect yourself from blame by assigning the victim as the cause of a bad outcome; check the roles and mechanisms.
149. Do not draw a stable trait from a single instance of behavior, especially in a new or coercive situation.
150. Do not justify the costs by saying that the effort was already great; assess the current usefulness of the result.
151. Don’t assume that safety measures don’t change behavior and don’t create new risks.
152. Don’t choose the smaller immediate benefit without a clear comparison with the larger deferred benefit.
153. Don’t choose an idea for its novelty; check its applicability, transition costs, and evidence.
154. Don’t give one specific victim more weight than a comparable number of victims without an explicit ethical rule.
155. Do not strengthen the obligation due to previous decisions; define the termination and review threshold in advance.
156. Do not automatically consider a standard unit to be an appropriate volume, limit, or target for this task.
157. Do not hold an asset just because its purchase price is known; compare the future expectation with the alternative.
158. Do not pass off a conditional guarantee as an unconditional one; check what must be true for the promised result.
159. Do not take a difficult‑to‑read argument as more profound or stronger; check the content separately from the presentation.
160. Don’t respond to advice with the opposite choice just because it restricts freedom; evaluate the advice yourself.
161. Don’t choose resistance to change as an end in itself; separate the defense of an important restriction from automatic protest.
162. Don’t compare yourself to an irrelevant group for the sake of self‑assessment; set a goal and a correct benchmark for comparison.
163. Don’t accept convenient information just because it’s available; evaluate whether additional information could change the decision.
164. Don’t assume that a simpler explanation is true by default; use simplicity as a criterion only after verifying the facts.
165. Don’t prefer a smaller whole to a larger set just because an individual element looks better; compare the overall value.
