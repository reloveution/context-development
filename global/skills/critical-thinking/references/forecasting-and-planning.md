# Estimates, forecasts, risks and plans

46. Don’t plan based solely on the best‑case scenario; compare the forecast with the actual results of similar tasks.
47. Don’t continue working just because you’ve already invested time, money, or effort; compare the future consequences of the options from scratch.
48. Don’t prefer the status quo without comparing the cost of inaction with the cost of change.
49. Don’t let the words “gain” and “loss” change your choice when the outcomes remain the same; rewrite the problem in both frames.
50. Do not evaluate an item, code, or solution that belongs to you using a more lenient scale than the external alternative.
51. Do not increase the assessment of the result just because personal effort has been invested in it; use independent quality criteria.
52. Do not accept “free” as having a zero price; calculate the time, attention, data, risks, and opportunity cost.
53. Do not let a shortage, timer, or artificial urgency replace the assessment of usefulness and condition checks.
54. Don’t change your preference due to the addition of a deliberately worse bait; compare the original options directly.
55. Don’t divide interchangeable resources into arbitrary “accounts” if this hides the overall result and opportunity cost.
56. Don’t prefer an immediate small effect to a larger future one without explicitly comparing the timeframe, risk, and value.
57. Don’t choose apparent safety just because one risk has become zero, ignoring the overall risk and benefit.
58. Don’t replace a decision with an action for the sake of a sense of control; check whether the action changes the mechanism or only creates the appearance of influence.
59. Before making an irreversible choice, fix the criteria, alternatives, and stopping conditions; don’t adjust them to fit a known outcome.
130. Don’t shift the time of an event in memory to a convenient date; verify the chronology against the records.
131. Don’t remember the past as unequivocally better or worse; compare the retained assessments with the current retelling.
132. Don’t evaluate a decision based on a past result if luck or external events could have changed the outcome.
133. Don’t confuse the moral assessment of the consequences with an assessment of the quality of the original decision.
134. Don’t describe a long‑term trend based on a few recent observations; check the full period and alternative series.
135. Don’t make a pessimistic forecast without comparing it with the baseline frequency and actual counterparts.
136. Don’t assume that reducing the task time linearly reduces its cost or risk.
137. Don’t accept a new decision as the best just because it’s new; compare maturity, risks, and evidence.
138. Don’t extrapolate from current self‑control to the future; take into account fatigue, incentives, and the conditions of refusal.
139. Don’t defend compatibility with a previous opinion at the cost of ignoring new data.
140. Don’t convey confidence in order to appear more competent; choose accuracy and verifiable uncertainty.
141. Don’t assess easy tasks with the same confidence as difficult ones; match your confidence with the complexity and past accuracy.
142. Don’t consider your own result above average without a suitable comparison group and measurement.
143. Don’t consider a future outcome better just because you hope for it; check the scenarios and frequencies.
